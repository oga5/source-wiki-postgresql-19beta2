1  <sect1 id="functions-datetime">
2   <title>Date/Time Functions and Operators</title>
3
4  <para>
5   <xref linkend="functions-datetime-table"/> shows the available
6   functions for date/time value processing, with details appearing in
7   the following subsections.  <xref
8   linkend="operators-datetime-table"/> illustrates the behaviors of
9   the basic arithmetic operators (<literal>+</literal>,
10   <literal>*</literal>, etc.).  For formatting functions, refer to
11   <xref linkend="functions-formatting"/>.  You should be familiar with
12   the background information on date/time data types from <xref
13   linkend="datatype-datetime"/>.
14  </para>
15
16  <para>
17   In addition, the usual comparison operators shown in
18   <xref linkend="functions-comparison-op-table"/> are available for the
19   date/time types.  Dates and timestamps (with or without time zone) are
20   all comparable, while times (with or without time zone) and intervals
21   can only be compared to other values of the same data type.  When
22   comparing a timestamp without time zone to a timestamp with time zone,
23   the former value is assumed to be given in the time zone specified by
24   the <xref linkend="guc-timezone"/> configuration parameter, and is
25   rotated to UTC for comparison to the latter value (which is already
26   in UTC internally).  Similarly, a date value is assumed to represent
27   midnight in the <varname>TimeZone</varname> zone when comparing it
28   to a timestamp.
29  </para>
30
31  <para>
32   All the functions and operators described below that take <type>time</type> or <type>timestamp</type>
33   inputs actually come in two variants: one that takes <type>time with time zone</type> or <type>timestamp
34   with time zone</type>, and one that takes <type>time without time zone</type> or <type>timestamp without time zone</type>.
35   For brevity, these variants are not shown separately.  Also, the
36   <literal>+</literal> and <literal>*</literal> operators come in commutative pairs (for
37   example both <type>date</type> <literal>+</literal> <type>integer</type>
38   and <type>integer</type> <literal>+</literal> <type>date</type>); we show
39   only one of each such pair.
40  </para>

1082  <para>
1083   When adding an <type>interval</type> value to (or subtracting an
1084   <type>interval</type> value from) a <type>timestamp</type>
1085   or <type>timestamp with time zone</type> value, the months, days, and
1086   microseconds fields of the <type>interval</type> value are handled in turn.
1087   First, a nonzero months field advances or decrements the date of the
1088   timestamp by the indicated number of months, keeping the day of month the
1089   same unless it would be past the end of the new month, in which case the
1090   last day of that month is used.  (For example, March 31 plus 1 month
1091   becomes April 30, but March 31 plus 2 months becomes May 31.)
1092   Then the days field advances or decrements the date of the timestamp by
1093   the indicated number of days.  In both these steps the local time of day
1094   is kept the same.  Finally, if there is a nonzero microseconds field, it
1095   is added or subtracted literally.
1096   When doing arithmetic on a <type>timestamp with time zone</type> value in
1097   a time zone that recognizes DST, this means that adding or subtracting
1098   (say) <literal>interval '1 day'</literal> does not necessarily have the
1099   same result as adding or subtracting <literal>interval '24
1100   hours'</literal>.
1101   For example, with the session time zone set
1102   to <literal>America/Denver</literal>:
1103<screen>
1104SELECT timestamp with time zone '2005-04-02 12:00:00-07' + interval '1 day';
1105<lineannotation>Result: </lineannotation><computeroutput>2005-04-03 12:00:00-06</computeroutput>
1106SELECT timestamp with time zone '2005-04-02 12:00:00-07' + interval '24 hours';
1107<lineannotation>Result: </lineannotation><computeroutput>2005-04-03 13:00:00-06</computeroutput>
1108</screen>
1109   This happens because an hour was skipped due to a change in daylight saving
1110   time at <literal>2005-04-03 02:00:00</literal> in time zone
1111   <literal>America/Denver</literal>.
1112  </para>
1113
1114  <para>
1115   Note there can be ambiguity in the <literal>months</literal> field returned by
1116   <function>age</function> because different months have different numbers of
1117   days.  <productname>PostgreSQL</productname>'s approach uses the month from the
1118   earlier of the two dates when calculating partial months.  For example,
1119   <literal>age('2004-06-01', '2004-04-30')</literal> uses April to yield
1120   <literal>1 mon 1 day</literal>, while using May would yield <literal>1 mon 2
1121   days</literal> because May has 31 days, while April has only 30.
1122  </para>

1943   <para>
1944    In these expressions, the desired time zone <replaceable>zone</replaceable> can be
1945    specified either as a text value (e.g., <literal>'America/Los_Angeles'</literal>)
1946    or as an interval (e.g., <literal>INTERVAL '-08:00'</literal>).
1947    In the text case, a time zone name can be specified in any of the ways
1948    described in <xref linkend="datatype-timezones"/>.
1949    The interval case is only useful for zones that have fixed offsets from
1950    UTC, so it is not very common in practice.
1951   </para>

1954    The syntax <literal>AT LOCAL</literal> may be used as shorthand for
1955    <literal>AT TIME ZONE <replaceable>local</replaceable></literal>, where
1956    <replaceable>local</replaceable> is the session's
1957    <varname>TimeZone</varname> value.
1958   </para>

1960   <para>
1961    Examples (assuming the current <xref linkend="guc-timezone"/> setting
1962    is <literal>America/Los_Angeles</literal>):
1963<screen>
1964SELECT TIMESTAMP '2001-02-16 20:38:40' AT TIME ZONE 'America/Denver';
1965<lineannotation>Result: </lineannotation><computeroutput>2001-02-16 19:38:40-08</computeroutput>
1966SELECT TIMESTAMP WITH TIME ZONE '2001-02-16 20:38:40-05' AT TIME ZONE 'America/Denver';
1967<lineannotation>Result: </lineannotation><computeroutput>2001-02-16 18:38:40</computeroutput>
1968SELECT TIMESTAMP '2001-02-16 20:38:40' AT TIME ZONE 'Asia/Tokyo' AT TIME ZONE 'America/Chicago';
1969<lineannotation>Result: </lineannotation><computeroutput>2001-02-16 05:38:40</computeroutput>
1970SELECT TIMESTAMP WITH TIME ZONE '2001-02-16 20:38:40-05' AT LOCAL;
1971<lineannotation>Result: </lineannotation><computeroutput>2001-02-16 17:38:40</computeroutput>
1972SELECT TIMESTAMP WITH TIME ZONE '2001-02-16 20:38:40-05' AT TIME ZONE '+05';
1973<lineannotation>Result: </lineannotation><computeroutput>2001-02-16 20:38:40</computeroutput>
1974SELECT TIME WITH TIME ZONE '20:38:40-05' AT LOCAL;
1975<lineannotation>Result: </lineannotation><computeroutput>17:38:40</computeroutput>
1976</screen>

1978    displays the value using the current <varname>TimeZone</varname>
1979    setting.  The second example shifts the time stamp with time zone value
1980    to the specified time zone, and returns the value without a time zone.
1981    This allows storage and display of values different from the current
1982    <varname>TimeZone</varname> setting.  The third example converts
1983    Tokyo time to Chicago time.  The fourth example shifts the time stamp
1984    with time zone value to the time zone currently specified by the
1985    <varname>TimeZone</varname> setting and returns the value without a
1986    time zone.  The fifth example demonstrates that the sign in a POSIX-style
1987    time zone specification has the opposite meaning of the sign in an
1988    ISO-8601 datetime literal, as described in <xref linkend="datatype-timezones"/>
1989    and <xref linkend="datetime-appendix"/>.

2028   <title>Current Date/Time</title>
2029
2030   <indexterm>
2031    <primary>date</primary>
2032    <secondary>current</secondary>
2033   </indexterm>
2034
2035   <indexterm>
2036    <primary>time</primary>
2037    <secondary>current</secondary>
2038   </indexterm>
2039
2040   <para>
2041    <productname>PostgreSQL</productname> provides a number of functions
2042    that return values related to the current date and time.  These
2043    SQL-standard functions all return values based on the start time of
2044    the current transaction:
2045<synopsis>
2046CURRENT_DATE
2047CURRENT_TIME
2048CURRENT_TIMESTAMP
2049CURRENT_TIME(<replaceable>precision</replaceable>)
2050CURRENT_TIMESTAMP(<replaceable>precision</replaceable>)
2051LOCALTIME
2052LOCALTIMESTAMP
2053LOCALTIME(<replaceable>precision</replaceable>)
2054LOCALTIMESTAMP(<replaceable>precision</replaceable>)
2055</synopsis>
2056    </para>
2057
2058    <para>
2059     <function>CURRENT_TIME</function> and
2060     <function>CURRENT_TIMESTAMP</function> deliver values with time zone;
2061     <function>LOCALTIME</function> and
2062     <function>LOCALTIMESTAMP</function> deliver values without time zone.
2063    </para>
2064
2065    <para>
2066     <function>CURRENT_TIME</function>,
2067     <function>CURRENT_TIMESTAMP</function>,
2068     <function>LOCALTIME</function>, and
2069     <function>LOCALTIMESTAMP</function>
2070     can optionally take
2071     a precision parameter, which causes the result to be rounded
2072     to that many fractional digits in the seconds field.  Without a precision parameter,
2073     the result is given to the full available precision.
2074    </para>
2075
2076   <para>
2077    Some examples:
2078<screen>
2079SELECT CURRENT_TIME;
2080<lineannotation>Result: </lineannotation><computeroutput>14:39:53.662522-05</computeroutput>
2081SELECT CURRENT_DATE;
2082<lineannotation>Result: </lineannotation><computeroutput>2019-12-23</computeroutput>
2083SELECT CURRENT_TIMESTAMP;
2084<lineannotation>Result: </lineannotation><computeroutput>2019-12-23 14:39:53.662522-05</computeroutput>
2085SELECT CURRENT_TIMESTAMP(2);
2086<lineannotation>Result: </lineannotation><computeroutput>2019-12-23 14:39:53.66-05</computeroutput>
2087SELECT LOCALTIMESTAMP;
2088<lineannotation>Result: </lineannotation><computeroutput>2019-12-23 14:39:53.662522</computeroutput>
2089</screen>
2090   </para>
2091
2092   <para>
2093    Since these functions return
2094    the start time of the current transaction, their values do not
2095    change during the transaction. This is considered a feature:
2096    the intent is to allow a single transaction to have a consistent
2097    notion of the <quote>current</quote> time, so that multiple
2098    modifications within the same transaction bear the same
2099    time stamp.
2100   </para>
2101
2102   <note>
2103    <para>
2104     Other database systems might advance these values more
2105     frequently.
2106    </para>
2107   </note>
2108
2109   <para>
2110    <productname>PostgreSQL</productname> also provides functions that
2111    return the start time of the current statement, as well as the actual
2112    current time at the instant the function is called.  The complete list
2113    of non-SQL-standard time functions is:
2114<synopsis>
2115transaction_timestamp()
2116statement_timestamp()
2117clock_timestamp()
2118timeofday()
2119now()
2120</synopsis>
2121   </para>
2122
2123   <para>
2124    <function>transaction_timestamp()</function> is equivalent to
2125    <function>CURRENT_TIMESTAMP</function>, but is named to clearly reflect
2126    what it returns.
2127    <function>statement_timestamp()</function> returns the start time of the current
2128    statement (more specifically, the time of receipt of the latest command
2129    message from the client).
2130    <function>statement_timestamp()</function> and <function>transaction_timestamp()</function>
2131    return the same value during the first statement of a transaction, but might
2132    differ during subsequent statements.
2133    <function>clock_timestamp()</function> returns the actual current time, and
2134    therefore its value changes even within a single SQL statement.
2135    <function>timeofday()</function> is a historical
2136    <productname>PostgreSQL</productname> function.  Like
2137    <function>clock_timestamp()</function>, it returns the actual current time,
2138    but as a formatted <type>text</type> string rather than a <type>timestamp
2139    with time zone</type> value.
2140    <function>now()</function> is a traditional <productname>PostgreSQL</productname>
2141    equivalent to <function>transaction_timestamp()</function>.
2142   </para>
2143
2144   <para>
2145    All the date/time data types also accept the special literal value
2146    <literal>now</literal> to specify the current date and time (again,
2147    interpreted as the transaction start time).  Thus,
2148    the following three all return the same result:
2149<programlisting>
2150SELECT CURRENT_TIMESTAMP;
2151SELECT now();
2152SELECT TIMESTAMP 'now';  -- but see tip below
2153</programlisting>
2154   </para>
2155
2156    <tip>
2157     <para>
2158      Do not use the third form when specifying a value to be evaluated later,
2159      for example in a <literal>DEFAULT</literal> clause for a table column.
2160      The system will convert <literal>now</literal>
2161      to a <type>timestamp</type> as soon as the constant is parsed, so that when
2162      the default value is needed,
2163      the time of the table creation would be used!  The first two
2164      forms will not be evaluated until the default value is used,
2165      because they are function calls.  Thus they will give the desired
2166      behavior of defaulting to the time of row insertion.
2167      (See also <xref linkend="datatype-datetime-special-values"/>.)
2168     </para>
2169    </tip>

2174
2175   <indexterm>
2176    <primary>pg_sleep</primary>
2177   </indexterm>
2178   <indexterm>
2179    <primary>pg_sleep_for</primary>
2180   </indexterm>
2181   <indexterm>
2182    <primary>pg_sleep_until</primary>
2183   </indexterm>

2187   <indexterm>
2188    <primary>delay</primary>
2189   </indexterm>
2190
2191   <para>
2192    The following functions are available to delay execution of the server
2193    process:
2194<synopsis>
2195pg_sleep ( <type>double precision</type> )
2196pg_sleep_for ( <type>interval</type> )
2197pg_sleep_until ( <type>timestamp with time zone</type> )
2198</synopsis>
2199
2200    <function>pg_sleep</function> makes the current session's process
2201    sleep until the given number of seconds have
2202    elapsed.  Fractional-second delays can be specified.
2203    <function>pg_sleep_for</function> is a convenience function to
2204    allow the sleep time to be specified as an <type>interval</type>.
2205    <function>pg_sleep_until</function> is a convenience function for when
2206    a specific wake-up time is desired.
2207    For example:
2208
2209<programlisting>
2210SELECT pg_sleep(1.5);
2211SELECT pg_sleep_for('5 minutes');
2212SELECT pg_sleep_until('tomorrow 03:00');
2213</programlisting>
2214   </para>
2215
2216   <note>
2217     <para>
2218      The effective resolution of the sleep interval is platform-specific;
2219      0.01 seconds is a common value.  The sleep delay will be at least as long
2220      as specified. It might be longer depending on factors such as server load.
2221      In particular, <function>pg_sleep_until</function> is not guaranteed to
2222      wake up exactly at the specified time, but it will not wake up any earlier.
2223     </para>
2224   </note>
2225
2226   <warning>
2227     <para>
2228      Make sure that your session does not hold more locks than necessary
2229      when calling <function>pg_sleep</function> or its variants.  Otherwise
2230      other sessions might have to wait for your sleeping process, slowing down
2231      the entire system.
2232     </para>
2233   </warning>
